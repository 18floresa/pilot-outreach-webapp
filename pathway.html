Steps to Become a Pilot

Becoming a pilot is a structured journey that blends education, aviation training, and hands-on experience. This page breaks down the steps toward becoming an airline pilot.

1. Meet the Basic Eligibility Requirements

Age Requirements:

  • 17+ for a Private Pilot License (PPL)
  • 18+ for a Commercial Pilot License (CPL)
  • 23+ for an Airline Transport Pilot License (ATP)

Education Requirements:

  • High school diploma (minimum)
  • A college degree is not required by the FAA, but many pilots earn one because:
    • It strengthens airline applications
    • Some airlines may reinstate degree requirements in the future
    • Provides a fallback career option if flying becomes impossible

FAA Medical Certificate:

  • Must hold a First-Class Medical Certificate
  • Must pass an FAA exam with an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME)
  • Required for airline pilots; failure to pass disqualifies you from airline pilot employment

2. Attend Flight School (Part 61 or Part 141)

Part 61 Pathway (How It Works)

1. Examples of Part 61 Flight Schools:

  • Local airport flight schools (most operate under Part 61)
  • AeroGuard Flight Training Center
  • Sierra Charlie Aviation

2. Train at a Flexible, Self-Paced Schedule

  • Students train at their own pace — ideal for balancing work or school
  • Instruction is individualized
  • Students can choose their instructor and schedule

3. Typically More Affordable

  • Part 61 programs cost less than university or academy programs
  • Students usually pay as they go, reducing large upfront costs

4. Earn the Same FAA Licenses and Ratings

  • PPL, IR, CPL, ME, and additional ratings
  • Training is less structured than Part 141 but leads to the same FAA standards

Part 141 Pathway (How It Works)

1. Attend a Part 141 Flight Training Program

Programs can be universities or independent flight schools.

Examples of University Part 141 Programs:
  • Ohio State University Aviation
  • Western Michigan University
Examples of Non-University Part 141 Schools:
  • ATP Flight School
  • CAE (American Airlines Cadet Academy)
  • Hillsboro Aero Academy

2. Enroll in Your Aviation Pathway

  • Training follows a structured FAA-approved syllabus
  • University programs may include a bachelor's degree + pilot ratings (PPL, IR, CPL, ME, etc.)
  • Non-university programs provide the ratings without the degree component

3. Complete All Coursework & Training

  • Graduates may qualify for a Restricted ATP (R-ATP) at 1,000 or 1,250 hours
  • This is lower than the standard 1,500 hours

4. Get Hired by a Regional Airline

  • Start building turbine time
  • Meet performance requirements

5. Transition to a Major Airline

  • After required experience, apply or flow to airlines such as United

3. Key Differences (Part 61 vs Part 141)

Training Structure:

  • Part 61: Flexible, train at your own pace
  • Part 141: Structured FAA-approved curriculum with stage checks

Type of School:

  • Part 61: Local flight schools and FBOs
  • Part 141: Universities and professional academies

Scheduling:

  • Part 61: Highly flexible, customized
  • Part 141: More rigid, follows a set syllabus

Cost:

  • Part 61: Usually cheaper, pay-as-you-go
  • Part 141: Often more expensive (especially at universities)

Time to Certificates:

  • Part 61: May take longer due to self-paced training
  • Part 141: Faster progression and reduced minimum hours

Restricted ATP Eligibility:

  • Part 61: Must meet full 1,500 hours
  • Part 141: Eligible at 1,000 or 1,250 hours

Best For:

  • Part 61: Students wanting flexibility or lower cost
  • Part 141: Students wanting a structured university or academy program

4. Build Flight Hours and Obtain Your ATP Certificate

To become an airline pilot, you must build experience and meet the FAA requirement of a minimum of 1,500 flight hours, which can be reduced through an approved Part 141 program. After reaching this milestone, you must also pass the exams for the Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) certificate.

5. Join a Regional Airline

Overview:

Regional airlines operate shorter routes for major carriers and hire early career pilots. They serve as the main entry point for pilots who eventually want to fly for airlines like United, Delta, or American.

1. Apply to Regional Airlines

  • SkyWest
  • Envoy
  • Piedmont
  • PSA
  • Republic
  • GoJet
  • CommuteAir

2. Interview

  • Includes technical questions
  • Successful applicants receive a Conditional Job Offer (CJO)

3. Airline Paid Training

  • ATP-CTP course
  • Aircraft systems ground school
  • Simulator sessions
  • Jet type rating (E175, CRJ, etc.)
  • Training typically takes 6–12 weeks

4. Pass All Checkrides

  • Required to officially become an airline pilot at a regional

5. First Officer (FO) Line Flying

  • Fly passengers as a First Officer
  • Follow airline procedures
  • Complete Initial Operating Experience (IOE)
  • Build turbine and 121 flight time

6. Build Experience

  • 1–3 years of turbine and airline (Part 121) flying
  • Master professional airline operations
  • Build strong safety record
  • Required for upgrading to Captain or applying to majors

7. Final Career Paths

Upgrade to Captain:

  • Build turbine time and leadership experience
  • Become competitive for major airlines

Direct to Major:

  • Some high-performing FOs are hired directly by United, Delta, American, etc.

Flow Programs:

  • Envoy → American Airlines
  • Endeavor → Delta Air Lines
  • Aviate → United Airlines
  • Allows transition without reapplying or reinterviewing
