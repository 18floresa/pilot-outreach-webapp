Steps to Become a Pilot

Becoming a pilot is a structured journey that blends education, aviation training, and hands-on experience. This page breaks down the steps toward becoming an airline pilot

1. Meet the Basic Eligibility Requirements

Age Requirements: 17+ for a Private Pilot License (PPL) 18+ for a Commercial Pilot License (CPL) 23+ for an Airline Transport Pilot License (ATP) Education Requirements: High school diploma (minimum) A college degree is not required by the FAA, but many pilots earn one because: It strengthens airline applications Some airlines may reinstate degree requirements in the future It provides a fallback career option if flying becomes impossible FAA Medical Certificate Must hold a First-Class Medical Certificate Must pass an FAA exam with an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME) Required for airline pilots and failure to pass a First-Class Medical means you cannot qualify for airline pilot employment

2. Attend Flight School (Part 61 or Part 141)

You must learn to fly through two approved flight training programs Part 61 Pathway (How It Works) Attend a Part 61 Flight Training Program 1. Examples of Part 61 Flight Schools: Local airport flight schools (most operate under Part 61) AeroGuard Flight Training Center Sierra Charlie Aviation 2. Train at a Flexible, Self-Paced Schedule Part 61 allows students to train at their own pace, making it ideal for those balancing work, college, or other responsibilities. Instruction is individualized, and students can choose their instructor and schedule. 3. Typically More Affordable Many Part 61 programs cost less than university or academy programs. Students usually pay as they go, reducing large upfront costs. 4. Earn the Same FAA Licenses and Ratings Part 61 students earn the same certifications: PPL, IR, CPL, ME, and additional ratings. Training is less structured than Part 141 but leads to the same FAA testing standards. Part 141 Pathway (How It Works) 1. Attend a Part 141 Flight Training Program Part 141 programs can be either universities or independent flight schools. Examples of University Part 141 Programs: Ohio State University Aviation Western Michigan University Examples of Non-University Part 141 Schools ATP Flight School CAE (American Airlines Cadet Academy) Hillsboro Aero Academy 2. Enroll in Your Aviation Pathway Flight training follows a structured FAA-approved syllabus. If you attend a university Part 141 program, you may graduate with both a bachelor's degree and your pilot ratings (PPL, IR, CPL, ME, etc.). If you attend a non-university Part 141 program, you complete the same ratings without the degree component. 3. Complete All Coursework & Training Graduates of approved Part 141 schools can qualify for the Restricted ATP (R-ATP) at 1,000 or 1,250 hours, instead of the standard 1,500 hours. 4. Get Hired by a Regional Airline Start your professional flying career and build turbine experience. Stay at the regional airline long enough to meet flight time and performance requirements. 5. Transition to a Major Airline After gaining the required experience, you can apply or flow to a major airline such as United.

3. Key differences

Part 61 vs Part 141: Key Differences Training Structure: Part 61: Flexible, train at your own pace. Part 141: Structured FAA-approved curriculum with stage checks. Type of School: Part 61: Common at local flight schools and FBOs. Part 141: Offered by universities and larger professional flight academies. Scheduling: Part 61: Very flexible, customized to your availability. Part 141: More rigid schedule, follows a set lesson plan. Cost: Part 61: Usually cheaper, pay-as-you-go. Part 141: Often more expensive, especially at university programs. Time to Certificates: Part 61: May take longer because pacing is student-driven. Part 141: Can finish faster due to structured syllabus and reduced minimum hours for some ratings. Restricted ATP Eligibility: Part 61: Must meet the full 1,500 hours for ATP. Part 141: Some programs qualify pilots for a Restricted ATP at 1,000 or 1,250 hours. Learning Environment: Part 61: Personalized, one-on-one instruction. Part 141: More formal training environment similar to airline-style operations. Best For: Part 61: Students who want flexibility, lower cost, or training while in school/work. Part 141: Students who want a structured, fast-paced path or plan to attend an aviation university.

4. Build Flight Hours and Obtain Your ATP Certificate/h2>

To become an airline pilot, you must build experience and meet the FAA requirement of a minimum of 1,500 flight hours, which can be reduced through an approved Part 141 program. After reaching this milestone, you must also pass the exams for the Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) certificate.

4. Join a Regional Airline

Regional airlines operate shorter routes for major carriers and hire early career pilots. Serve as the main entry point for pilots who want to eventually fly for airlines like United, Delta, or American. 1. Apply to Regional Airlines Pilots submit their flight time, training history, and resume to airlines like SkyWest, Envoy, Piedmont, PSA, Republic, GoJet, CommuteAir. 2. Interview Interviews include technical questions successful applicants receive a Conditional Job Offer (CJO) that depends on them passing training to get the job. 3. Airline Paid Training Upon susscuefl completion the the regional airline will pay for your training including: ATP-CTP course Aircraft systems ground school Simulator sessions Type rating for the jet (E175, CRJ,) Training typically takes 6-12 weeks 4. Pass all Checkrides after Airline Paid training Checkrides happen after the interview, during airline training. Passing them is required to officially become an airline pilot at a regional. 5. First Officer (FO) Line Flying After passing the checkride, pilots begin flying passengers as First Officers. They operate regional jets, follow airline procedures, complete Initial Operating Experience, and build turbine and 121 flight time needed to move to major airlines. 6. Build Experience After starting as a First Officer, pilots spend 1-3 years logging turbine and airline (Part 121) time, mastering professional airline operations, and building a strong safety record. This experience is required for upgrading to Captain or applying to major airlines. 7.Final Career Paths: Upgrade, Direct Hire, Flow Program Upgrade to Captain: Spend time building up turbine time and leadership experience, making pilots extremely competitive for major airlines. Direct to Major: Some high-performing First Officers are hired directly by airlines like United, Delta, or American without upgrading to Captain, depending on experience, record, and hiring demand. Flow program: Flow programs create a direct pathway from regional airlines to major carriers. Those in flow programs (Envoy to American, Endeavor to Delta, Aviate to United) have structured pathways. Pilots hired at certain regionals can move to the major airline such as American, Delta, or United without reapplying or reinterviewing, as long as they meet performance and service requirements. These programs remove uncertainty and provide a clear route to a mainline career.
