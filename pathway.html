Steps to Become a Pilot

Becoming a pilot is a structured journey that blends education, aviation training, and hands-on experience. This page breaks down the steps toward becoming an airline pilot.

1. Meet the Basic Eligibility Requirements

Age Requirements:

  • 17+ for a Private Pilot License (PPL)
  • 18+ for a Commercial Pilot License (CPL)
  • 23+ for an Airline Transport Pilot License (ATP)

Education Requirements:

  • High school diploma (minimum)
  • A college degree is not required but strengthens airline applications
  • Some airlines may reinstate degree requirements in the future
  • College provides a fallback career option if flying becomes impossible

FAA Medical Certificate:

  • Must hold a First-Class Medical Certificate
  • Must pass an FAA exam with an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME)
  • Failure to pass disqualifies you from airline pilot employment

2. Attend Flight School (Part 61 or Part 141)

Part 61 Pathway (How It Works)

  • Local airport flight schools (most operate under Part 61)
  • AeroGuard Flight Training Center
  • Sierra Charlie Aviation

Key Features of Part 61:

  • Flexible, self-paced training schedule
  • Individualized instruction
  • Lower cost; pay-as-you-go model
  • Earn the same ratings: PPL, IR, CPL, ME, etc.

Part 141 Pathway (How It Works)

Examples of University Part 141 Programs:

  • Ohio State University Aviation
  • Western Michigan University

Examples of Non-University Part 141 Schools:

  • ATP Flight School
  • CAE (American Airlines Cadet Academy)
  • Hillsboro Aero Academy

Key Features of Part 141:

  • Structured FAA-approved syllabus
  • University programs may include a bachelor's degree + ratings
  • Students may qualify for the Restricted ATP at 1,000–1,250 hours
  • Faster, more formal training environment

3. Key Differences: Part 61 vs Part 141

Training Structure:

  • Part 61: Flexible, train at your own pace
  • Part 141: Structured curriculum with stage checks

Type of School:

  • Part 61: Local flight schools and FBOs
  • Part 141: Universities and large academies

Scheduling:

  • Part 61: Customized, highly flexible
  • Part 141: Fixed syllabus and schedule

Cost:

  • Part 61: Usually cheaper
  • Part 141: Often more expensive (especially universities)

Time to Certificates:

  • Part 61: Can take longer depending on student pace
  • Part 141: Faster progression; reduced minimum hours

Restricted ATP Eligibility:

  • Part 61: Full 1,500 hours required
  • Part 141: Eligible for 1,000 or 1,250 hour R-ATP

Best For:

  • Part 61: Students needing flexibility or lower cost
  • Part 141: Students wanting a structured, fast-paced path

4. Build Flight Hours and Obtain Your ATP Certificate

To become an airline pilot, you must build experience and meet the FAA requirement of a minimum of 1,500 flight hours (reduced only through a Part 141 R-ATP program). You must then pass the exams for the Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) certificate.

5. Join a Regional Airline

What Regional Airlines Do:

Regional airlines operate shorter routes and serve as the entry point for early-career pilots.

Steps in the Regional Airline Journey:

  • Apply: SkyWest, Envoy, Piedmont, PSA, Republic, GoJet, CommuteAir
  • Interview: Technical and HR questions; receive a Conditional Job Offer (CJO)
  • Paid Training: ATP-CTP course, systems training, simulator sessions, type rating
  • Checkrides: Must pass to officially become a First Officer
  • Line Flying: Fly passengers under airline procedures and build turbine/121 time
  • Upgrade or Flow: Become Captain or transition to a major airline through a flow program (Aviate → United)
