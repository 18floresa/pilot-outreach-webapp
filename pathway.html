Steps to Become a Pilot

Becoming a pilot is a structured journey that blends education, aviation training, and hands-on experience. This page breaks down the steps toward becoming an airline pilot.

Age Requirements:

  • 17+ for a Private Pilot License (PPL)
  • 18+ for a Commercial Pilot License (CPL)
  • 23+ for an Airline Transport Pilot License (ATP)

Education Requirements:

  • High school diploma (minimum)
  • A college degree is not required by the FAA, but many pilots earn one because:
    • It strengthens airline applications
    • Some airlines may reinstate degree requirements in the future
    • It provides a fallback career option if flying becomes impossible

FAA Medical Certificate:

  • Must hold a First-Class Medical Certificate
  • Must pass an FAA exam with an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME)
  • Required for airline pilots and failure to pass a First-Class Medical means you cannot qualify for airline pilot employment

You must learn to fly through two approved flight training programs.

Part 61 Pathway (How It Works)

Attend a Part 61 Flight Training Program

1. Examples of Part 61 Flight Schools:

  • Local airport flight schools (most operate under Part 61)
  • AeroGuard Flight Training Center
  • Sierra Charlie Aviation

2. Train at a Flexible, Self-Paced Schedule

  • Part 61 allows students to train at their own pace.
  • Instruction is individualized, and students can choose their instructor and schedule.

3. Typically More Affordable

  • Many Part 61 programs cost less than university or academy programs.
  • Students usually pay as they go.

4. Earn the Same FAA Licenses and Ratings

  • PPL, IR, CPL, ME, and additional ratings.

Part 141 Pathway (How It Works)

1. Attend a Part 141 Flight Training Program

Part 141 programs can be universities or independent flight schools.

Examples of University Part 141 Programs:

  • Ohio State University Aviation
  • Western Michigan University

Examples of Non-University Part 141 Schools:

  • ATP Flight School
  • CAE (American Airlines Cadet Academy)
  • Hillsboro Aero Academy

2. Enroll in Your Aviation Pathway

  • Structured FAA-approved syllabus.
  • University programs may include a bachelor's degree.

3. Complete All Coursework & Training

  • Eligible for R-ATP at 1,000 or 1,250 hours.

4. Get Hired by a Regional Airline

  • Build turbine experience.

5. Transition to a Major Airline

  • Apply or flow to a major airline.

Training Structure:

  • Part 61: Flexible, self-paced.
  • Part 141: Structured with stage checks.

Type of School:

  • Part 61: Local flight schools and FBOs.
  • Part 141: Universities + professional academies.

Scheduling:

  • Part 61: Very flexible.
  • Part 141: Rigid class-style schedule.

Cost:

  • Part 61: Usually cheaper.
  • Part 141: Often more expensive.

Time to Certificates:

  • Part 61: Takes longer.
  • Part 141: Faster progression.

Restricted ATP Eligibility:

  • Part 61: Must reach 1,500 hours.
  • Part 141: Qualifies for R-ATP reductions.

Learning Environment:

  • Part 61: Personalized instruction.
  • Part 141: Airline-style training.

To become an airline pilot, you must build experience and meet the FAA requirement of a minimum of 1,500 flight hours, which can be reduced through an approved Part 141 program. After reaching this milestone, you must also pass the exams for the Airline Transport Pilot (ATP) certificate.

Regional airlines operate shorter routes for major carriers and hire early career pilots.

1. Apply to Regional Airlines

Pilots submit their flight time, training history, and resume to airlines like SkyWest, Envoy, Piedmont, PSA, Republic, GoJet, and CommuteAir.

2. Interview

Interviews include technical questions. Successful applicants receive a Conditional Job Offer (CJO) that depends on them passing training.

3. Airline Paid Training

Upon successful completion, the regional airline will pay for your training, including:

  • ATP-CTP course
  • Aircraft systems ground school
  • Simulator sessions
  • Type rating for the jet (E175, CRJ)

Training typically takes 6–12 weeks.

4. Pass All Checkrides After Airline Paid Training

Checkrides happen after the interview and during airline training. Passing them is required to officially become an airline pilot at a regional.

5. First Officer (FO) Line Flying

After passing the checkride, pilots begin flying passengers as First Officers. They operate regional jets, follow airline procedures, complete Initial Operating Experience, and build turbine and Part 121 flight time needed to move to major airlines.

6. Build Experience

After starting as a First Officer, pilots spend 1–3 years logging turbine and airline (Part 121) time, mastering professional airline operations, and building a strong safety record. This experience is required for upgrading to Captain or applying to major airlines.

7. Final Career Paths: Upgrade, Direct Hire, Flow Program

Upgrade to Captain: Spend time building turbine PIC time and leadership experience, making pilots extremely competitive for major airlines.

Direct to Major: Some high-performing First Officers are hired directly by airlines like United, Delta, or American without upgrading to Captain, depending on experience, record, and hiring demand.

Flow Programs:

  • Provide a direct pathway from regional airlines to major carriers
  • Examples: Envoy → American, Endeavor → Delta, Aviate → United
  • No reapplication or re-interview once in the program
  • Pilots must meet performance and service requirements
  • Creates a predictable path to a mainline career
